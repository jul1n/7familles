# The 7 Families of Dams
*French Committee on Large Dams (CFBR)*

Website : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/

A dam card game in the spirit of Happy Families (Quartett) by the French Committee on Large Dams (CFBR, 1926–2026): 7 families, 42 illustrated cards and fact sheets.

> The 7 Families of Dams is a card game in the spirit of “Happy Families” (known as “Quartett” in German): you ask the other players for cards to complete whole families. It is similar, but a little different: each family has 6 cards instead of 4, there is a draw pile as in “Go Fish”, and every card teaches you something about dams. ([About Happy Families (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Happy_families))

## Rules of the game

You collect cards to form complete families, and you learn about dams along the way. The printed game is currently available in French only; this page is a translation of its rules.

**Age** : 4 and up · **Players** : 2 to 4 · **Time** : 15 to 20 min

### Contents

- 42 cards split into 7 families of 6 cards
- 1 rules card and 1 information card
- 1 card presenting the authors of the game

### Goal of the game

The goal of the game is to collect as many complete families as possible before the end of the game.

### How to play

1. Shuffle the cards and deal 7 to each player; the rest is placed face down on the table and forms the draw pile. The player to the left of the dealer starts by asking any player of their choice for a card to complete a family (for example: “In the JOBS family, I would like the Owner card.”). To ask for a card, the player must already hold at least one card of that family.
2. If the other player has that card, they must hand it over; otherwise, the asker must draw from the pile. If the drawn card is the right one, the asker says “good draw” and keeps going until they can no longer get the card they want, either from another player or from the pile: then their turn ends.
3. It is then the turn of the player on the left to ask for the cards they want. When a player holds a complete family (all 6 cards), they lay it face up in front of them, and the game goes on until there are no more families left to complete.

### End of the game

If the draw pile is empty, the game goes on until all the families have been collected. The winner is the player who has gathered the most families in front of them at the end of the game.

## Jobs

*The women and men who design, build and monitor dams.*

### 1. Owner

*The owner pays for the dam to be built, then looks after it for its whole life: upkeep, monitoring, safety.*

#### In brief
The owner is the dam's **guardian**. Just like the owner of a house, they pay to keep it standing and in good shape. Except that their "house" holds back millions of liters of water!

#### How does it work?
In France, many large dams belong to **the State**. It hands over their operation to a company through a contract called a **concession** (EDF, for example, produces electricity with them). Dams that supply tap water or water fields mostly belong to towns or groups of municipalities.

The owner must:
- **pay** for monitoring and repairs, year after year;
- **imagine the worst**: for the most important dams, they write and update a danger study (what would happen in a huge flood, when the river overflows, or in an earthquake?);
- **work with the State**, which checks that everything is safe for the people living downstream, that is, further down the valley.

#### Key facts
- Dams are ranked **A, B or C** depending on their height and the volume of water they hold (for small structures, what lies downstream also counts).
- Class A is the most closely controlled: it starts at **20 meters** high (a 7-story building), but height is not the only criterion.

#### Words to know
- **Concession**: a contract by which the State entrusts a company with building or running a hydroelectric facility, for a limited time.
- **Operator**: the one who runs the dam day to day (sometimes the owner, sometimes a concession company).
- **Downstream**: the side of the dam where the river carries on its way.

> **Did you know?**
> The biggest dams have an alert plan for local residents, with **sirens** tested regularly, so that everyone is ready if one day an evacuation were needed.

#### Going further
In France, dams are ranked A, B or C mainly according to their height and the amount of water they hold. The dams in the most important classes get more thorough checks and studies. Some very large facilities also have a special emergency plan (called a PPI) that organizes the alert and the protection of residents in case of an accident.

### 2. Designer

*She invents the shape of the dam and does all the calculations so that it can withstand the water and the ground.*

#### In brief
The designer is the dam's **architect-engineer**. Before the first digger arrives, she draws the dam and checks, with math and computers, that it meets the safety rules with generous margins.

#### How does it work?
She works in a design office, a team of engineers. She starts by asking: *which shape fits this valley best?* A curved wall? A huge pile of stones? A very heavy wall?

Then she uses some very clever tools:
- **A model inside the computer**: she makes water, cold, heat and even earthquakes "push" on a virtual dam, to see where it struggles.
- **Flood calculation**: she predicts the biggest flood possible, so that the overflow can be let out without going over the top.
- **Choosing materials**: which concrete, which soil, which rocks.

#### Key facts
A dam is calculated to withstand **extremely rare** floods, much bigger than the ones we know: the more important the structure, the more exceptional the reference flood.

#### Words to know
- **Concrete**: a mix of cement, sand, gravel and water that hardens like stone.
- **Flood**: when the river swells very strongly.

> **Did you know?**
> A French engineer, **André Coyne**, is one of the great pioneers of modern thin arch dams: curved walls that are thin and strong. His dams have been built on several continents.

#### Going further
For floods, engineers do not study only the ones already seen. They also calculate very rare floods to check that the dam and its spillway can cope with them. A so-called "thousand-year" flood has about a one-in-1,000 chance of happening each year: it does not necessarily come back every 1,000 years.

### 3. Builder

*He brings the dam out of the ground by following the plans, with hundreds of people and giant machines.*

#### In brief
The builder is the **giant site manager**. He turns the plans into a real dam, sometimes high in the mountains, in narrow gorges or in the middle of a river. Hundreds of people work with him.

#### How does it work?
Building a large concrete dam takes a lot of patience. Here is an example of the steps (they change depending on the type of dam):
1. **Divert the river.** You cannot build with your feet in the water! The river is first sent through a tunnel dug into the mountain, while a small temporary wall (a *cofferdam*) keeps it away.
2. **Dig down to solid rock.** Just like the foundations of a house, you need a very hard base.
3. **Pour the concrete**, in big blocks. Inside, small pipes of cold water cool it, because concrete heats up as it sets and could crack.

#### Key facts
- Depending on the dam, a large site lasts from **a few years to nearly ten years**.
- Depending on the case, **cable cars** are set up on site to carry equipment, along with a concrete plant and machines that crush rock.

#### Words to know
- **Cofferdam**: a temporary wall that keeps water away during the work.
- **Foundation**: the part that sinks into the ground to hold the dam up.

> **Did you know?**
> The Hoover Dam, in the United States, contains miles of cold-water pipes. Without them, the concrete would have taken **more than 100 years** to cool down and would have cracked!

#### Going further
The machines depend on the place and on the dam being built. In the high mountains, a cable car can carry workers and materials when no road reaches the site. For a large concrete dam, a concrete plant is often set up nearby to make huge amounts of it.

### 4. Safety expert

*He monitors the dam's health, like a doctor, to spot the slightest problem very early.*

#### In brief
The safety expert is the dam's **doctor**. He listens to it, measures it and watches over it, day and night, to spot a tiny problem long before it becomes serious. This work is called **monitoring** (or *auscultation*).

#### How does it work?
A dam is not completely still: it moves a tiny bit! It expands (gets slightly bigger) when it is hot, and shrinks when it is cold. The expert:
- reads the measurements of a **pendulum**: a long wire hanging inside the dam, which shows how far the wall moves, to a tenth of a millimeter;
- looks at the **pressure of the water** seeping under the dam;
- sends **divers** or small underwater robots to inspect the wall on the lake side.

#### Key facts
The dam is **monitored continuously** and visited regularly. The more important it is, the more frequent the checks and reports.

#### Words to know
- **Monitoring (auscultation)**: examining the dam with instruments, like a doctor with a stethoscope.
- **Seepage**: water that slowly slips into rock or soil.

> **Did you know?**
> Among the clues that are watched is the **water from the drains**, small pipes that carry away seepage: its quantity and its clarity are tracked (is it clear or cloudy?). If it turns cloudy, perhaps soil is being carried away: it must be checked quickly!

#### Going further
- Monitoring reports: every year (class A), every 3 years (class B), every 5 years (class C).
- Instrument reports: every 2 years (class A), every 5 years (classes B and C).
- On top of that come the visits and rules specific to each dam.
- Clear drain water is not enough to conclude that the dam is safe: the flow, the turbidity (is the water cloudy?) and how they change over time are also followed.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/metiers-expert-securite/

### 5. Hydrologist

*She studies rain, rivers and floods to know how much water is coming, and when.*

#### In brief
The hydrologist is **the scientist of the journey of water**. She follows water from the rain or snow down to the river, to predict floods (too much water) and droughts (not enough).

#### How does it work?
To make predictions, she investigates:
- **She measures**: stations placed in rivers measure the flow (the amount of water passing by) and radars follow the rain.
- **She compares with history**: what was the biggest flood in 100 years? In 1,000 years? The dam must be able to cope with it.
- **She looks to the future**: climate change alters snow, droughts and some very heavy rains, in different ways depending on the region. She helps manage water well for the decades to come.

#### Key facts
Flow is measured in **cubic meters per second** (m³/s). One cubic meter is 1,000 liters, like 10 full bathtubs. During big storms in the Cévennes, the flow of a small river can **increase enormously** in just a few hours!

#### Words to know
- **Watershed (drainage basin)**: the whole area where rain ends up in the same river.
- **Evaporation**: when the water of a lake flies off as vapor, thanks to the sun and wind.

> **Did you know?**
> A large lake can lose **several millimeters of water per day** in summer, just through evaporation. Hydrologists measure that too!

#### Going further
Hydrologists do not make a single prediction of the future: they compare several models. Depending on the region, climate change can affect rain, snow, droughts and floods in different ways. In France, the Explore2 project studies these changes basin by basin, and also shows what scientists still understand poorly.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/metiers-hydrologue/

### 6. Geologist

*Before the work starts, she studies the rocks and the ground to check that the terrain is solid enough.*

#### In brief
A dam is only as good as the ground it stands on! The geologist is **the rock specialist**. She explores the underground of the valley to be sure it will not give way, and will not let the water leak out.

#### How does it work?
From the very first studies, she:
- **drills holes** and brings up "cores": long cylinders of rock, to spot cracks and pockets of clay;
- **tests watertightness**: she injects pressurized water into the hole and watches whether it escapes;
- **plans a "grout curtain"**: a curtain of liquid cement pushed into small cracks, to block the paths of the water.

#### Key facts
For an arch-shaped dam, the cliffs on each side must bear an **enormous thrust**: the geologist checks that they are strong enough.

#### Words to know
- **Geologist**: a person who studies rocks and the history of the Earth.
- **Fault**: a large natural crack in the rock.

> **Did you know?**
> In 1959, the Malpasset dam (France) failed. It was not the concrete of the arch that gave way first: investigations pointed to the role of the **rock** it rested on and the water inside it. Since then, the rock and the underground water are studied much more carefully.

#### Going further
The choice of dam depends on several things: the shape of the valley, the strength and watertightness of the rock, the materials available, the floods to be passed and what the dam will be used for. A narrow valley with very solid rock may suit an arch dam well; a wider valley may suit an embankment dam better.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/metiers-geologue/

## Uses

*From drinking water to electricity: the many essential missions of reservoirs.*

### 1. Drinking water

*Dams store water that is then cleaned so that it is good to drink, to cook with and to wash with.*

#### In brief
When you turn on the tap, where does the water come from? Sometimes from a dam! These big lakes are like **giant water towers**. They keep water in reserve, even after months without rain.

#### How does it work?
1. **Protect the lake.** Around it, rules limit the activities that could pollute: the water must stay clean from the start.
2. **Pick the right water.** The intake (the entrance, like a giant straw) can draw water from several depths, to take the coolest and clearest water depending on the season.
3. **Clean it.** In a plant, it is filtered, the dirt is left to settle at the bottom (*settling*), then it is disinfected. It then travels in pipes to homes.

#### Key facts
In France, about **one third** of the water taken to become drinkable comes from surface water (rivers, lakes and dams). The other two thirds or so come from underground water.

#### Words to know
- **Drinkable (potable)**: good to drink, safe for health.
- **Disinfect**: to kill germs.

> **Did you know?**
> In Marseille, tap water comes from the Alps! It travels over **more than 100 kilometers** in canals, thanks to the Durance-Verdon system (to which the Serre-Ponçon dam contributes a lot), simply flowing downhill.

#### Going further
Water from a dam lake is never sent straight to the tap. Before it is distributed, it is treated, then checked regularly: we make sure it contains no germs or dangerous substances. That is also why the lake itself is protected: the cleaner the water is at the start, the easier it is to make drinkable.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/usages-eau-potable/

### 2. Hydroelectricity

*The dam's water turns turbines that make electricity, to light our homes, heat them and power our travel.*

#### In brief
The word is long, but the idea is simple: "hydro" means *water*. Hydroelectricity is **electricity made with falling water**. It is the world's first renewable source of electricity (an energy that never runs out).

#### How does it work?
Imagine a giant slide:
1. Water is stored **up high**, in the lake.
2. It rushes down big steel pipes, the **penstocks**, faster and faster.
3. It hits the blades of a **turbine**, a kind of big mill, which starts to spin.
4. The turbine turns a **generator**, which makes electricity, sent along wires to our homes.

#### Key facts
- Some lake power plants or pumped-storage stations can start very quickly, in **just a few minutes**!
- In France, hydropower provides about **1 electricity in 10** (11.4% in 2025) and gives off very few of the gases that warm the planet.

#### Words to know
- **Renewable**: something that renews itself (water comes back with the rain).
- **Turbine**: a wheel with blades that a fluid makes spin.

> **Did you know?**
> When everyone switches on the light in the evening, or when the sun sets and solar panels stop, hydro plants can **raise their output very fast**: they help keep the electric grid balanced.

#### Going further
A hydroelectric plant burns no fuel: it only uses falling water, so it gives off very few greenhouse gases while it runs. But building a dam takes a lot of concrete, steel and work, which leaves a footprint on the environment. That is why electricity sources are compared over their whole life, from construction to the end of the structure.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/usages-hydroelectricite/

### 3. Irrigation

*Dams keep water to water the fields and let plants grow, even in summer.*

#### In brief
Without water, plants do not grow! **Irrigation** means bringing water to fields, orchards and vegetable gardens. Dams set aside the water of winter rain and snow, to give it to crops in summer.

#### How does it work?
- Water is released into the river, or sent through **canals** and pipes to the fields.
- It arrives in clever watering systems, like **drip irrigation**, which can cut water losses when well installed and well adjusted.
- Thanks to these reserves, we do not need to pump too much from rivers and underground water (the water hidden in the soil) when they are almost dry.

#### Key facts
- Worldwide, agriculture makes up about **70%** of the fresh water taken (7 liters out of 10).
- In France, canals and reservoirs bring water to a great many hectares of crops.

#### Words to know
- **Crop**: plants we grow in order to harvest them.
- **Aquifer (water table)**: a reserve of water hidden under the ground.

> **Did you know?**
> The Serre-Ponçon lake makes it possible to water **more than 150,000 hectares** in Provence. Thanks to it, fruit and vegetables grow there even when it hardly rains!

#### Going further
Not all the water taken to water a field is "consumed". Some is drunk by the plants or evaporates into the air, while some goes back to the river or soaks into the ground to reach the aquifers. Specialists therefore tell apart the water that is **withdrawn** (the water we go and get) from the water that is truly **consumed** (the water that does not come back).

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/usages-irrigation/

### 4. Flow regulation

*The dam keeps or releases water to prevent floods and to stop the river from running dry.*

#### In brief
The **flow** is the amount of water passing in the river. It is never quite the same: sometimes the river overflows, sometimes it is almost dry. The dam acts as a **giant tap** to calm all that down.

#### How does it work?
It has two missions:
1. **Reduce floods.** When it rains very hard, some dams keep part of the lake empty, like a big bathtub. They store the extra water there, then release it very gently. Towns downstream are better protected, even though the risk never disappears.
2. **Help the river in summer.** When the river lacks water (this is called **low-water period**, or *étiage*), the dam releases a little water continuously. Fish survive, factories keep running and power plants stay cooled.

#### Key facts
The 4 big reservoir lakes upstream of Paris can hold **810 million cubic meters** of water, enough to fill 320,000 Olympic swimming pools, to reduce some floods of the Seine and support its flow in summer.

#### Words to know
- **Flood**: a river that swells and risks overflowing.
- **Low water (étiage)**: the period when the river is at its lowest.

> **Did you know?**
> During very dry summers, like in 2022, the Naussac and Villerest dams **release water** to help the Loire keep flowing. In some stretches, this supply can be a significant part of its flow.

#### Going further
To reduce a flood, a dam must have free space in its reservoir before the water arrives: a lake that is already full can no longer store anything. That is why some dams keep part of their volume empty, or lower their level a little when heavy rain is forecast. Even so, a dam reduces the risk of flooding without ever removing it.

### 5. Transport

*Thanks to dams and locks, boats can sail on rivers and big rivers.*

#### In brief
A wild river has strong currents and places too shallow for boats. Dams and canals turn it into a **motorway on water**, where barges carry huge loads while using little energy.

#### How does it work?
- Dams create **steps of calm water**, like a staircase, called *reaches* (*biefs*).
- To go from one step to the next, the boat enters a **lock**: a big box with a gate at each end. The gates are closed, the box is filled or emptied, and the boat **goes up or down** like in an elevator.
- The Rhine, the Rhône and the Seine link ports to big cities and factories in this way.

#### Key facts
A large convoy of 4,400 tonnes carries as much as **220 trucks**. For the same tonne carried over one kilometer, a boat generally emits less CO₂ (the gas that warms the planet) than a truck.

#### Words to know
- **Lock**: a boat elevator that works with water.
- **Barge**: a long flat boat that carries goods.

> **Did you know?**
> At the Three Gorges dam, in China, a real **boat elevator** lifts ships of 3,000 tonnes by **113 meters** (almost as high as a 35-story building), in under 40 minutes.

#### Going further
- The CO₂ balance depends on the boat, the load, the fuel and the trip: we say it is "generally favorable" per tonne-kilometer, without boiling it down to a single figure.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/usages-transport/

### 6. Tourism

*Dam lakes attract visitors who come to have fun and admire the scenery.*

#### In brief
By holding back water in the hollow of a valley, some dams create a **big lake** that has become a tourist site: people swim, go boating and walk there.

#### How does it work?
A calm lake in the middle of mountains or forests allows plenty of activities:
- **Water sports**: sailing, windsurfing, paddleboarding, canoeing and kayaking, rowing.
- **Swimming and relaxing**: supervised beaches, campsites and hiking trails around the lake.
- **Fishing**: pike, perch and trout live there.
- **A summer agreement**: on some lakes, the company that produces electricity promises to keep enough water from June to September, so that everyone can enjoy the activities.

#### Key facts
The lakes of Serre-Ponçon and Sainte-Croix (in the Verdon gorges) are **very well-known holiday destinations**.

#### Words to know
- **Nautical**: relating to activities on the water.
- **Hiking**: a long walk in nature.

> **Did you know?**
> If the water of some glacier lakes is turquoise, it is thanks to **"rock flour"**: tiny grains of rock, ground by glaciers, float in the water and send back the sunlight.

#### Going further
The level of a dam lake is not fixed: it can rise or fall by several meters depending on the seasons and uses (storing snowmelt, producing electricity, supporting the river in summer). Depending on the time of year, a beach or a jetty can therefore end up far from the water. To swim or sail safely, always follow the signs posted on site.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/usages-tourisme/

## Components

*The key parts that make a dam work and keep it safe.*

### 1. Dam body

*It is the big wall of the dam: it holds back all the water of the lake and passes the push on to the ground or the cliffs.*

#### In brief
The body is **the main wall** of the dam, the one you see across the valley. Whether it is made of concrete or of earth and stones, its job is simple: to **hold firm** against the enormous push of the water.

#### How does it work?
The deeper you go in a lake, the harder the water pushes (you have felt it in your ears at the bottom of a swimming pool!). So the dam body is built to:
- **be wider at the bottom than at the top** (as many dams are), where the push is strongest;
- **not let water through**: thanks to the mass of the concrete, or to a clay core (a very sticky soil) or to a special lining on the lake side;
- **be built in blocks**: large concrete dams are generally built in blocks and successive layers, because concrete expands and shrinks with temperature. Flexible joints between the blocks keep everything watertight.

#### Key facts
The push of the water on a large dam can reach **several million tonnes**.

#### Words to know
- **Watertight**: that does not let water through.
- **Clay**: a fine, sticky soil that blocks water.

> **Did you know?**
> Inside large concrete dams there are **lit galleries**, like submarine corridors. Technicians walk through them to inspect the dam from the inside!

#### Going further
- A gravity dam takes the push mostly through its weight and its foundation. An arch dam passes most of it on to its rocky abutments (the cliffs).
- The "wider at the bottom" shape does not apply to all types of dams.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/composants-corps/

### 2. Spillway

*It lets the extra water through so that the lake does not spill over the top of the dam.*

#### In brief
The spillway is the dam's **safety valve**, like the overflow of a bathtub. When a very big flood arrives, it lets the extra water go, to keep it from passing over the top of the dam.

#### How does it work?
Water falling from very high is very powerful. The spillway guides it in three steps:
1. **The sill**: the water spills over at the right spot, either on its own when it reaches a precise level (with no moving parts, which avoids some breakdowns), or by opening big gates called *sluice gates*.
2. **The chute**: a huge **concrete slide** that brings the water down gently.
3. **The energy dissipator**: at the bottom, the water is thrown by a **ski-jump bucket** in big sprays, or falls into a basin, to lose its strength.

#### Key facts
The spillway of Itaipu (Brazil and Paraguay) can pass about **62,000 cubic meters per second**, about 40 times the average flow of the Iguazu Falls, a huge waterfall very close by.

#### Words to know
- **Flood**: when the river swells very strongly.
- **Sluice gate**: a big door that opens or closes to control the water.

> **Did you know?**
> Some spillways are shaped like a **giant funnel** (they are called "morning glory" spillways). The water swirls there, plunges into a big hole, then comes out lower down, behind the dam.

#### Going further
There are two main families of spillways. The **free sill** works with no motor and no control: as soon as the lake rises above a precise level, the water goes over, which avoids breakdowns. The **gated** spillway has big gates that can be opened on demand to control the level of the lake, but it needs energy and upkeep. Many dams combine both.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/composants-evacuateur/

### 3. Foundation

*The ground the dam rests on: it holds it up and passes its weight on to the rock.*

#### In brief
The foundation is the dam's **hidden root**. Sunk into the riverbed and into the sides of the mountain, it bears the weight of the dam and the push of the lake.

#### How does it work?
The great enemy is not only the water you can see, it is the water that **sneaks under the dam**:
- **Uplift pressure**: this water pushes upward and would like to lift the dam, like a cork!
- **The grout curtain**: thousands of holes are drilled and liquid cement is injected to block all the cracks in the rock, down to several tens of meters deep, depending on the rock of the site.
- **The drains**: just behind, small pipes collect the water that gets through anyway, to lower the pressure safely.

#### Key facts
Depending on the site, people sometimes dig **several tens of meters** under the riverbed to find solid rock.

#### Words to know
- **Foundation**: a base sunk into the ground that supports a building or a dam.
- **Drain**: a small pipe that carries water away.

> **Did you know?**
> At Serre-Ponçon, the bedrock was under **more than 100 meters of sand and gravel**! Engineers built a deep watertight cut-off wall in these alluvial deposits (the sand and gravel laid down by the river) to limit leaks under the dam.

#### Going further
Water passing under a dam does not only leak: it presses upward and "lightens" the dam, a bit like a boat that floats. A lighter dam rubs less on its ground and resists sliding less well. That is why engineers install drains and constantly measure the water pressure in the foundation.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/composants-fondation/

### 4. Water intake

*It takes water from the lake and sends it where it is needed. The bottom outlet, next to it, makes it possible to lower the level of the lake.*

#### In brief
The water intake is the dam's **tap**. It is the big entrance through which water from the lake goes off to the turbines, the irrigation canals or the drinking-water plants.

#### How does it work?
It includes, among other things:
- **The screens**: big steel bars stop tree trunks, branches and debris, so that they do not damage the turbines.
- **The head gate**: a big safety door, which can cut off the water at once in case of a problem or for a repair.

Next to it, another structure is not used to take water: **the bottom outlet**, a huge pipe at the very bottom of the dam. It makes it possible to lower the level of the lake, even to empty it depending on the dam, and sometimes to flush out the silt (the sand and mud) piled up at the bottom.

#### Key facts
In a penstock, water can exceed **100 km/h** as it reaches the turbine, the speed of a car on a country road!

#### Words to know
- **Turbine**: a wheel that spins under the push of water to make electricity.
- **Sediments**: sand, mud and small stones that the river carries.

> **Did you know?**
> The bottom outlet is tested regularly. When its gate opens, the water gushes out with **so much force** that it would damage any protection: better not to stand in front of it!

#### Going further
- The water intake and the bottom outlet are two different structures. The intake feeds a pipe, a plant or a canal. The bottom outlet allows releases at low level, a partial or full emptying depending on the project, and sometimes the management of sediments.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/composants-prise-deau/

---

### 5. Sensors

*They measure movements, water pressure and leaks to watch over the dam's health.*

#### In brief
Sensors are the **senses of the dam**. Hidden in the concrete and the rock, hundreds of instruments measure day and night the slightest movements, the water pressure and the vibrations of the ground.

#### How does it work?
Engineers use plenty of surprising instruments:
- **Pendulums**: a long steel wire hung in a vertical shaft, sometimes tens of meters long. With great precision (of the order of a tenth or a hundredth of a millimeter), it shows how far the dam moves.
- **Piezometers**: they measure the water pressure inside the dam and in the ground.
- **Leak-measuring boxes**: small V-shaped boxes measure the flow of water running out of the drains.
- **Laser sightings and GPS**: they measure the movements of the top of the dam, to check that they stay normal.

#### Key facts
A 100-meter-high arch dam **moves forward by a few millimeters to a few centimeters** when the lake fills, then comes back when it empties. We say it "breathes"!

#### Words to know
- **Sensor**: a device that measures something (a pressure, a movement, a temperature...).
- **Optical fiber**: a very thin wire that carries light.

> **Did you know?**
> In some recent dams, **kilometers of optical fibers** have been buried in the concrete. They measure temperature and deformation all along the dam.

#### Going further
What matters most to the engineer is how a measurement changes over time. A single value says little: a displacement of a few millimeters can be perfectly normal in summer and worrying if it shows up in the middle of winter. So each measurement is compared with those of previous years, to spot a slow drift before it becomes a problem.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/composants-capteurs/

### 6. River

*It brings the water to the lake, then goes on its way after the dam: it must be protected, along with those who live in it.*

#### In brief
A dam does not remove the river: it **lives with it**! The river brings water (and sand) to the lake, then carries on behind the dam. The animals and plants that live there must be protected.

#### How does it work?
Engineers take care of **ecological continuity**: the river must remain a place of passage for everything that lives in it. To do this:
- **The minimum flow**: a minimum amount of water must keep flowing downstream, 365 days a year, for fish, insects and plants.
- **Fish passes**: water staircases, small artificial streams or even fish elevators let trout, salmon and eels get past the dam.
- **The passage of sand**: depending on the site, devices or operations are set up to let sand and gravel move down to the downstream side, so that fish find places to lay their eggs.

#### Key facts
In France, in the general case, the law requires leaving at least **one tenth of the river's average flow** after the dam (one twentieth in some cases).

#### Words to know
- **Upstream / downstream**: upstream = before the dam, lake side; downstream = after it, river side.
- **Migratory fish**: fish that travel between the sea and rivers (salmon, eel).

> **Did you know?**
> At the Golfech dam, on the Garonne, a current of water attracts migratory fish into a big cage. It **lifts them up like an elevator** to the other side of the dam!

#### Going further
A dam holds back part of the sand and gravel that the river carries: they settle at the bottom of the lake. Downstream, the river may then run short of them, its bed slowly deepens and fish find fewer places to spawn. That is why operations are sometimes organized to let these sediments move on downstream.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/composants-riviere/

## Types of structures

*Embankment, gravity, arch, canals or pumped storage: the designs suited to each valley.*

### 1. Embankment dam

*It is made of packed earth and rock: a big "hill" that holds back the water.*

Location : Mont-Cenis dam · Photo credit : © Benoit Blancher

#### In brief
The embankment dam looks like **a mountain built by humans**. It is made of piled-up earth or stones, with gentle slopes. It is the most common type of dam in the world, because it fits almost every valley.

#### How does it work?
Instead of a straight wall, it has the shape of a **big spread-out triangle**:
- **The "shoulders"**: millions of tonnes of rocks and gravel, which give the dam its weight and stability.
- **Watertightness**: since rocks let water through, a barrier is needed. Either a **clay core** (a sticky soil) in the middle, or a concrete or bitumen **facing** on the lake side.
- **The filters**: layers of graded sand stop the water from carrying the soil away.

#### Key facts
- It is the family of dams that is **the most widespread** in the world.
- **Mont-Cenis (Savoie)**: 120 meters high, and a lake at 2,000 m altitude.

#### Words to know
- **Embankment (remblai)**: a pile of earth or rocks packed down to form a wall or a hill.
- **Crest**: the top of the dam.

> **Did you know?**
> For a standard embankment dam, water passing over the top (called **overtopping**) is a major risk: it can tear the soil away in a few minutes. That is why a well-sized spillway is planned.

#### Going further
In an earth dam, a little water always ends up getting through: what matters is that it does not carry the soil with it. The **filters**, layers of sorted sand and gravel, let the water through but hold back the grains of soil; the **drains** collect this water and send it downstream. Without good filters, a tiny passage could widen from the inside.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/types-barrage-remblai/

---

### 2. Gravity dam

*A wall of concrete or stone: its enormous weight keeps it from being pushed by the water.*

Location : Moulin Neuf dam · Photo credit : © Rémy Tourment

#### In brief
The gravity dam is **the champion of brute force**. It is a wall of concrete or stone so heavy that it holds mainly through its weight, helped by its foundation, without sliding or tipping under the push of the water.

#### How does it work?
Think of a big block of stone at the edge of a table: to push it, you need a lot of force!
- The water **pushes** the dam downstream.
- Its **weight** presses it to the ground, with an even bigger force.
- Result: it stays in place. Its shape, wide at the bottom and narrower at the top, looks like a **right triangle**: the lake side is almost straight, the downstream side is sloped.

#### Key facts
The tallest gravity dam in the world is **the Grande Dixence** (Switzerland): **285 meters** high, with **15 million tonnes of concrete**.

#### Words to know
- **Masonry**: construction with stones joined with mortar.
- **RCC (roller-compacted concrete)**: almost dry concrete, spread by bulldozers and crushed by rollers.

> **Did you know?**
> Today, many gravity dams are made of **roller-compacted concrete**: it is spread like soil, then packed down. It makes building much faster!

#### Going further
In a gravity dam, the foundation and the drainage are almost as important as the weight of the concrete. If water seeps under the dam, it lifts it a little and reduces the friction that holds it on the ground. So drains are drilled under the structure, and the water pressure is monitored to make sure it stays low.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/types-barrage-poids/

---

### 3. Arch dam

*Its curved shape lets it hold back water by leaning on the cliffs on each side.*

Location : Quinson dam · Photo credit : © Franck Oddoux, Parker Wayne Philips

#### In brief
The arch dam is a dam that is **curved** toward the lake, like an arch. It does not hold mainly through its weight, but by **passing the push of the water on to the cliffs** on each side of the valley.

#### How does it work?
It is the same principle as the arches of old bridges or churches, but laid down horizontally:
- The water pushes on the curved wall, which **squeezes itself together** instead of bending.
- Concrete loves being squeezed! The force follows the curve and leans on the rock on both sides: these supports are called **abutments**.
- Modern arches are also curved from top to bottom (*double curvature*), like the hull of a boat, which makes them very thin.

#### Key facts
- An arch dam can use **far less concrete** than a comparable gravity dam.
- **Quinson (Verdon)**: a pretty arch 45 meters high.

#### Words to know
- **Arch (voûte)**: a curved shape, like a bow.
- **Abutment**: a support of rock or concrete that receives the push.

> **Did you know?**
> When the lake fills, the water pushes harder and the arch **squeezes more tightly against its supports**. Engineers calculate these forces and deformations for every water level.

#### Going further
An arch uses little concrete, but in exchange it needs a fairly narrow valley and very solid rock supports, because all the push of the water ends up in the cliffs. That is why it is built mostly in gorges. In a wide valley, an embankment dam or a gravity dam is often better suited.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/types-barrage-voute/

---

### 4. Canals

*Rivers dug by humans to bring water from one place to another and let boats sail.*

Location : Ille-et-Rance Canal · Photo credit : © Chris De Lompret

#### In brief
A canal is a **river made by humans**. It is dug across plains and hills to carry goods, bring water to towns or irrigate fields.

#### How does it work?
A river runs downhill on its own. A canal, on the other hand, is more like a **staircase of flat stretches of water**:
- **The reach (bief)**: a very watertight stretch, where the water stays calm and at a constant level, so that barges move without fighting a current.
- **The locks**: to climb a hill, boats go from lock to lock, like in an elevator.
- **The canal bridges**: to cross a river or a valley, a bridge is built that carries a canal! The most famous in France is the one at Briare, above the Loire.

#### Key facts
- **Ille-et-Rance Canal** (Brittany): 85 km and 48 locks between Rennes and Saint-Malo, opened in 1832.
- France has about **8,500 km** of navigable waterways (canals and improved rivers), one of the longest networks in Europe.

#### Words to know
- **Reach (bief)**: a section of canal between two locks.
- **Barge**: a long, flat boat for carrying goods.

> **Did you know?**
> The highest point of a canal (the *summit reach*) is tricky to feed with water, because each lockage lets water flow down. It is supplied with **reservoirs**, channels or pumps, otherwise the canal would dry up!

#### Going further
Every passage through a lock uses water: like filling a big box, all the water needed to lift a boat then flows away downstream. So the canal must be fed all the time, especially at its highest point. Some locks have **water-saving basins**, neighboring reservoirs that recover part of this water to give it back to the next passage.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/types-canaux/

### 5. Flood protection levee

*A long structure that protects residents and land against floods.*

Location : Doménon levee · Photo credit : © Rémy Tourment

#### In brief
A dam blocks a river to make a lake. A levee, instead, is **a long wall that runs along the river or the sea**, sometimes for kilometers, to stop water from flooding towns and fields.

#### How does it work?
- **When all is well**: the levee is dry, or the water barely touches its foot.
- **When there is a flood**: the water rises, and the levee keeps it in its bed.
- **The risks being watched**:
  - *overtopping*: water goes over the top and gnaws at the levee from behind;
  - *piping (the "hydraulic fox")*: water slips under the levee and carries away sand, like a small tunnel that keeps getting bigger;
  - *sliding*: the levee, soaked with water, loses strength and slides.
- **A trick**: sometimes a deliberately lower spot is planned where water can spill over toward fields, to **protect the houses**.

#### Key facts
In France, **thousands of kilometers of levees** protect millions of people, along the Loire, the Rhône, the Seine and the coast.

#### Words to know
- **Flood (inundation)**: when water covers land that is normally dry.
- **Levee (digue)**: a long wall or bank that holds back water.

> **Did you know?**
> The burrows of coypus, badgers or foxes can weaken a levee. That is why workers walk along it regularly, especially after a flood, to spot and fill in these holes.

#### Going further
A levee reduces a risk but does not remove it: it is designed to protect up to a certain level of flood. If a bigger flood arrives, the water can go over the top or the levee can fail. That is why we avoid building right behind a levee, and why residents of protected areas must stay informed about the risks.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/types-digue-protection/

### 6. Pumped-storage station (PSH)

*It pumps water uphill when there is too much electricity, then lets it fall back down to produce some when there is not enough.*

Location : Revin pumped-storage station · Photo credit : © Jean Louis Burnod

#### In brief
In French, STEP stands for **Station de Transfert d'Énergie par Pompage** (pumped-storage station). It is like **a giant battery**: electricity is stored in the form of water held high up, and recovered when it is needed.

#### How does it work?
A pumped-storage station has two reservoirs at different altitudes: one high up, one low down.
1. **When there is surplus electricity** (at night, or in full sun when solar panels produce a lot): pumps **lift the water** from the lower lake to the upper lake. The extra electricity is put aside!
2. **When there is a shortage** (for example at 7 p.m. in winter, when everyone switches on the light): the gates are opened. The water **comes back down** and spins turbines, which make electricity in less than 3 minutes.

#### Key facts
- Pumped-storage stations are one of the main ways to store very large amounts of energy.
- **Grand'Maison** (Isère): the most powerful in Europe, **1,800 MW**.
- **Revin** (Ardennes): 800 MW.

#### Words to know
- **MW (megawatt)**: a unit of power. 1 MW is 1,000 kW, the power of about 1,000 radiators of 1 kW.
- **Efficiency**: what you get back compared with what you put in.

> **Did you know?**
> The idea is not new: the first pumped-storage stations were built in Italy and Switzerland at the end of the 19th century, more than 120 years ago!

#### Going further
A pumped-storage station does not create energy: it uses some to pump the water uphill, then recovers about 70 to 80% of it when the water comes back down. What it brings is the ability to **store** very large amounts of electricity, for hours, and give them back when the grid needs them.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/types-step/

## Around the world

*The giants of hydropower and waterways on a planetary scale.*

### 1. Itaipu Dam

*Built on the Paraná River, it produces electricity for Brazil and Paraguay, which share it.*

Location : Brazil and Paraguay · Photo credit : © Deni Williams

#### In brief
Itaipu is built on the Paraná River, **on the border between Brazil and Paraguay**, two countries that built it together. It is one of the **biggest electricity producers in the world**, thanks to its 20 enormous turbines.

#### How does it work?
Itaipu combines a big concrete dam with long earth embankments on the sides:
- **20 giant pipes** 10.5 meters across (a double-decker bus would fit through!) send the water onto turbines that weigh nearly 300 tonnes each.
- **Two electrical "languages"**: Paraguay uses current at 50 hertz and Brazil at 60 hertz (the *hertz* measures how fast the current "goes back and forth"). So half of the turbines spin one way, the other half the other way.

#### Key facts
- **Power**: 14,000 MW.
- In 2016, Itaipu produced **103 billion kWh**: that year, this covered about 85% of Paraguay's electricity and 15% of Brazil's (these shares change from one year to the next).
- **Length**: 7.9 km. **Height**: 196 m.

#### Words to know
- **kWh (kilowatt-hour)**: an amount of electricity. An electric oven switched on for 1 hour uses about 2.
- **Binational**: belonging to two countries.

> **Did you know?**
> The dam is very close to the **Iguazu Falls**, one of the biggest waterfalls in the world. In case of a very big flood, its spillway can let through about 40 times the average flow of these falls!

#### Going further
Itaipu is run by a joint company owned in equal parts by Brazil and Paraguay. The electricity produced is shared in two equal halves. Since Paraguay uses less than its half, it sells the rest to Brazil: that is how the dam supplies a lot of electricity to both countries.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/monde-itaipu/

### 2. Suez Canal

*It lets ships go from the Mediterranean to the Red Sea without sailing all the way around Africa.*

Location : Egypt · Photo credit : © Axelspace Corporation

#### In brief
Dug through the desert, between Africa and Asia, the Suez Canal is a **giant shortcut** between the Mediterranean Sea and the Red Sea. Without it, ships would have to sail around Africa: more than **7,000 km** extra!

#### How does it work?
The Suez Canal has a special feature: **it has no locks at all**!
- The Mediterranean and the Red Sea are almost at the **same level**. Seawater therefore flows freely from one end to the other, like in a big trench filled with water.
- It is a **dug and dredged channel** (cleared of the sand that builds up in it) in sand and clay. It passes through a large lake, where ships can pass each other.

#### Key facts
- **Length**: 193 km, from Port Said to Suez.
- **Inaugurated** in a big ceremony on 17 November 1869, after 10 years of work. The project was led by the Frenchman Ferdinand de Lesseps.
- About **one tenth** of the world's maritime trade passes through it.

#### Words to know
- **Lock**: a boat elevator that makes up for a difference in water level.
- **Container ship**: a very large ship that carries giant metal boxes.

> **Did you know?**
> In March 2021, a huge 400-meter container ship, the *Ever Given*, ended up **across the canal** because of the wind. It stayed stuck for six days, and more than 400 ships had to wait!

#### Going further
No locks, but two permanent challenges. The first is sand: the desert wind keeps depositing it, and the canal must be dredged continuously to keep its depth. The second is traffic: over a large part of the canal there is only one lane, so ships travel in convoys and pass each other in widened areas, like the Great Bitter Lake.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/monde-canal-suez/

### 3. Grande Dixence Dam

*It holds back the water of the Swiss mountains to produce electricity in power plants.*

Location : Switzerland · Photo credit : © Jérémy Toma

#### In brief
At 2,365 meters above sea level, in the Swiss Alps, the Grande Dixence is **the tallest gravity dam in the world**. This gigantic concrete wall holds back the meltwater of dozens of glaciers.

#### How does it work?
To fill this lake of 400 million cubic meters, the Swiss did a titanic job:
- Under the mountain, **100 kilometers of galleries** collect the water of 35 glaciers, including those near Zermatt and the Matterhorn (a tooth-shaped mountain), to bring it to the lake.
- The water is then sent to the Bieudron power plant down a very high drop of **1,883 meters**, one of the highest in the world for a plant of this type. It hits turbines among the most powerful in the world.

#### Key facts
- **Height**: 285 m, almost the Eiffel Tower (330 m with the antenna).
- **Concrete**: 15 million tonnes.
- The dam was built from 1951 to 1961 (the project was completed later); each winter, work stopped for months because of frost and snow.

#### Words to know
- **Glacier**: an enormous mass of ice that moves very slowly.
- **Altitude**: height above sea level.

> **Did you know?**
> At the bottom of the lake lies **an old dam**, the Dixence dam, built in the 1930s. When the big dam was filled, the small one was swallowed up, and it still sleeps there!

#### Going further
The lake of the Grande Dixence is not filled only by the valley right behind the dam. A network of galleries and water intakes also collects water from torrents in several neighboring valleys, sometimes by pumping it, and brings it to the lake. A dam is therefore not always fed by "its" river alone.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/monde-grande-dixence/

### 4. Kariba Dam

*It forms an immense lake by holding back the Zambezi River, between Zambia and Zimbabwe.*

Location : Zambia and Zimbabwe · Photo credit : © Manfidza

#### In brief
Built in the 1950s on the Zambezi River, in Africa, Kariba gave birth to one of the **largest artificial lakes in the world**, often cited among the top by volume of water. The dam is a big arch, designed by the engineers Coyne and Bellier, under the impetus of André Coyne.

#### How does it work?
Kariba is an **arch dam**: it is curved, and the push of the river is sent onto the rocky walls of the gorge.
- Two power plants, one on each side of the river (one in Zambia, one in Zimbabwe), supply the region and its copper mines.

#### Key facts
- The lake can hold about **180 billion cubic meters of water**: about four times more than the lake of the Three Gorges!
- **Height**: 128 m. **Length**: 579 m.
- The lake covers more than **5,500 km²**, about the size of a French department.

#### Words to know
- **Zambezi**: a great river of southern Africa, famous for the Victoria Falls.
- **Mine**: a place where ores, like copper, are extracted.

> **Did you know?**
> During the construction, the river had some very big floods. In the tradition of the Tonga, a people living along the river, it was the anger of **Nyaminyami**, the river's serpent god. The engineers held on and finished the dam!

#### Going further
Kariba is run jointly by two neighboring countries separated by the river: Zambia and Zimbabwe. A joint authority between the two countries manages the lake and the dam. Each country has its own power plant, on its own bank of the Zambezi.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/monde-kariba/

### 5. Three Gorges Dam

*On the Yangtze River, it is the most powerful hydroelectric plant in the world.*

Location : China · Photo credit : © Claudio Carvajal

#### In brief
On the Yangtze, China's longest river, the Three Gorges Dam is **the most powerful hydroelectric plant in the world**, with 22.5 GW installed (1 GW = 1 billion watts). It is one of the biggest construction projects ever carried out.

#### How does it work?
It fulfills three missions at once:
1. **Produce electricity**: 32 enormous units of 700 MW each, plus two small ones.
2. **Protect against floods**: before the dam, Yangtze floods caused tens of thousands of victims. The lake can hold back **22 billion cubic meters** of flood water.
3. **Allow navigation**: five locks in a staircase and a **boat elevator** 113 meters high let 3,000-tonne cargo ships travel up to Chongqing.

#### Key facts
- **Installed power**: 22,500 MW, the power of about twenty nuclear reactors of around 1,000 MW each.
- **Length**: 2,335 m. **Height**: 185 m.
- **Concrete**: 28 million cubic meters, poured between 1994 and 2009.

#### Words to know
- **Nuclear reactor**: a machine that produces electricity from the energy of uranium.
- **Cargo ship**: a big ship that carries goods.

> **Did you know?**
> The lake's water is so heavy (nearly 40 billion tonnes) that, according to NASA calculations, it may have **lengthened the day by about 0.06 microsecond** (a microsecond is a millionth of a second). Almost nothing, but it is an estimate by NASA!

#### Going further
Installed power tells what a plant can produce at most at a given moment, a bit like the top speed of a car. The energy produced in a year depends on the water available: a dry year gives less than a wet one. A plant therefore almost never runs at full power all year long.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/monde-trois-gorges/

### 6. Hoover Dam

*It holds back the Colorado River to produce electricity and supply water across the region.*

Location : United States · Photo credit : © USBR

#### In brief
In a canyon, on the border between Nevada and Arizona, the Hoover Dam is one of the **most famous dams in the world**. It was built in the 1930s, during a hard time for the United States (the "Great Depression"). This big public project put thousands of people to work and created a major piece of infrastructure for the American West.

#### How does it work?
It is an **arch-gravity dam**: it is curved like an arch and also very heavy.
- Its curve sends the push of the Colorado River toward the canyon walls, and its base, 200 meters thick, makes it heavy and stable.
- It created **Lake Mead**, one of the largest artificial lakes in the United States. This lake is an important water reserve for the Colorado River basin, and the dam's electricity supplies several states in the region.
- Its style is **Art Deco**, with sculpted towers.

#### Key facts
- **Height**: 221 m. **Length**: 379 m.
- **Power**: 2,080 MW.
- Inaugurated in **1935**; the work was finished more than 2 years ahead of the contract.

#### Words to know
- **Canyon**: a very narrow, deep valley carved by a river.
- **Art Deco**: a decoration style that was very fashionable in the 1920s and 1930s.

> **Did you know?**
> Near the dam, two big bronze statues 9 meters tall watch over the site. Their **toes have turned shiny**, because visitors touch them for good luck!

#### Going further
When Lake Mead drops during a drought, two things go down at the same time: the water reserve, and the height of the drop. And the higher the water falls from, the more energy it gives the turbines. With a lower lake, the plant therefore produces less electricity from the same amount of water.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/monde-hoover-dam/

## In France

*The masterpieces of French water heritage, from mainland France to the overseas territories.*

### 1. Serre-Ponçon Dam

*A dam with many uses: it forms a tourist lake, produces electricity, irrigates fields and tames the Durance.*

Location : Provence-Alpes-Côte d'Azur · Photo credit : © Bernard Gaëtan

#### In brief
Where the Durance and the Ubaye meet, Serre-Ponçon is a **major structure of the Durance system**. Before it, the Durance was nicknamed the "third scourge of Provence", because of its floods. Today, this earth dam is used to produce electricity, irrigate, supply water and for tourism.

#### How does it work?
Serre-Ponçon is an **embankment dam**, with a story of ingenuity:
- Under the river, there was a buried canyon, filled with **more than 100 meters of sand and gravel** that let water through.
- Engineers first built, underground, **a very deep watertight cut-off wall** in these alluvial deposits, then raised the dam: a mountain of **14 million cubic meters** of earth and rock.
- Its power plant, dug underground, produces electricity, then the water leaves in a canal that feeds 15 other power plants all the way to the Étang de Berre.

#### Key facts
- **The lake**: about 1.2 billion cubic meters, enough to fill nearly 500,000 Olympic swimming pools.
- **Height**: 123 m. **Commissioned** in 1960.

#### Words to know
- **Confluence**: a place where two rivers join.
- **Scourge**: a great misfortune that keeps coming back.

> **Did you know?**
> In the middle of the lake, you can see the tiny **Saint-Michel chapel**, on a small island. It recalls the landscape from before, when the villages of Savines and Ubaye were swallowed up when the lake was filled.

#### Going further
Serre-Ponçon is the largest artificial lake of mainland France by volume. But in French Guiana, the Petit-Saut lake is even larger, with about 3.5 billion cubic meters.

### 2. Migouélou Dam

*It holds back the mountain water in the Pyrenees to produce electricity in power plants.*

Location : Occitanie (Hautes-Pyrénées) · Photo credit : © CFBR

#### In brief
At **2,278 meters altitude**, in the Pyrenees National Park, Migouélou is a very original high-mountain dam. It is made of **nine small concrete arches** that follow the shape of the terrain.

#### How does it work?
Building this high was a challenge for the engineers. Since all the cement had to be carried up by **cable car**, they chose a light dam:
- **9 thin arches** that lean on **8 buttresses** (concrete pillars that serve as supports).
- The stored water feeds, through galleries dug in the rock, a series of **seven power plants** in the Azun valley.

#### Key facts
- **Altitude**: 2,278 m, in the high mountains.
- **Height**: 31 m. **Length**: 274 m.
- Built between 1956 and 1958, **commissioned in 1959**.

#### Words to know
- **Buttress**: a pillar that supports a wall from behind or from the side.
- **Cable car**: a cabin or bucket hung from a cable.

> **Did you know?**
> No road goes up to the dam! Workers, machines (taken apart in pieces) and materials were carried up by **cable car**. Today, it generally takes **several hours of hiking**, depending on the route, to visit it.

#### Going further
A multiple-arch dam is not one big single arch: it is several thin arches, side by side, that each pass the push of the water on to buttresses, the concrete pillars. These pillars then send the push into the rock. This saves concrete, which was precious when everything had to be carried up by cable car.

### 3. Rance Tidal Power Station

*Between Saint-Malo and Dinard, it uses the movement of the tides to produce electricity.*

Location : Brittany (Ille-et-Vilaine) · Photo credit : © Yannick Le Gal

#### In brief
Between Saint-Malo and Dinard, the Rance power station was the **world's first tidal power station** to produce electricity on a large scale. It uses the force of the **tides**, that is, the sea rising and falling, because of the pull of the Moon and the Sun.

#### How does it work?
In Saint-Malo, the sea rises and falls by an enormous height:
- **Rising tide**: the sea enters the estuary (the mouth of the river) through the dam's turbines.
- **Falling tide**: the dam holds the water back. When the sea has gone down far enough, the water is let out again, and it spins the turbines the other way.
- **Very special turbines**: 24 reversible "bulb" units, adapted to the tidal station, spin in both directions and can also pump water for extra help.

#### Key facts
- **Maximum tide**: up to 13.5 m difference between high tide and low tide.
- **Power**: 240 MW.
- **Production**: about 500 million kWh per year, enough to supply a city like Rennes.
- Inaugurated in **1966** by General de Gaulle.

#### Words to know
- **Tide**: the regular rise and fall of the sea, twice a day.
- **Estuary**: a large mouth where a river flows into the sea.

> **Did you know?**
> The top of the dam is also **a 4-lane road**: thousands of cars use it every day to go from Dinard to Saint-Malo without taking the long way around!

#### Going further
Tides can be predicted very long in advance: we know the time and height of the tide for years to come, because they depend on the movement of the Moon and the Sun. That is not the case for wind or sunshine, which change with the weather. It is a rare advantage for a renewable energy.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/france-rance/

### 4. Grand Canal d'Alsace

*It makes it possible to sail on the Rhine between Basel and Strasbourg and to produce electricity.*

Location : Grand Est · Photo credit : © Didier Marc, Parker Wayne Philips

#### In brief
The Grand Canal d'Alsace is a **canal about 50 kilometers long** that runs alongside the Rhine, from Kembs to Vogelgrun. It is part of the waterway between Basel and Strasbourg. It is used both to **let boats sail** and to **produce electricity**.

#### How does it work?
In the 20th century, France decided to develop the Rhine, a river that was then wild:
- A dam (at Kembs) **diverts part of the river** into a very wide canal, up to 150 meters.
- Along the canal, the water passes through **four lock-and-power stations**: Kembs, Ottmarsheim, Fessenheim and Vogelgrun.
- Each site has a power plant and **two big locks** to let boats through without interruption.

#### Key facts
- **Length**: about 50 km, from Kembs to Vogelgrun.
- **Production**: about 4.5 billion kWh per year, with hardly any air pollution.
- Convoys of **3,000 tonnes** can link Basel to Rotterdam.

#### Words to know
- **Convoy**: a group of barges (flat boats) pushed together by a single boat.
- **Rhine**: a great river that crosses Switzerland, France, Germany and the Netherlands.

> **Did you know?**
> **Fish passes** have been installed on the Rhine to help migratory fish, like the Atlantic salmon, swim back up the river: they help restore the continuity of the Rhine.

#### Going further
Fish passes improve the continuity of the river, but they are not enough on their own. For a salmon to swim up the Rhine, it must get past all the obstacles on the river, from the sea to the rivers where it spawns. The return of migratory fish therefore depends on the whole river, not on a single dam.

### 5. Loire Levees

*Built along the Loire, these levees protect towns and houses against floods.*

Location : Centre-Val de Loire & Pays de la Loire · Photo credit : © Rémy Tourment

#### In brief
Over more than **600 kilometers**, the Loire levees run along the "royal river". These levees, begun in the Middle Ages, protect hundreds of towns and villages against floods.

#### How does it work?
The protection of the Loire was built little by little:
- As early as the 12th century, the first earth levees were built, called **turcies**.
- In the 17th and 19th centuries, they were raised to **more than 6 meters** above the valley (the plain where the river can overflow).
- To keep the levee from failing in a town, **spillways** (*déversoirs*) were planned: lower spots, in masonry, where the water overflows on purpose toward fields, to save towns like Orléans, Blois or Tours.

#### Key facts
- **600 km** of levees along the river.
- They protect residents, activities and territories (of the order of 300,000 people). The Loire Valley is also on the UNESCO World Heritage list for its cultural landscape (a list of places to protect for all humanity).
- Reference big floods: **1846, 1856 and 1866**.

#### Words to know
- **Levee**: an earth bank that runs along a river.
- **Spillway (déversoir)**: a place where water can overflow on purpose.

> **Did you know?**
> The Loire levees are also used as a **path**: the famous cycle route "La Loire à Vélo" often follows them!

#### Going further
When a levee breaks, water can flood the plain behind it very quickly, because this plain is lower than the river. That is the great danger of these levees, and the reason they are monitored and maintained all the time. The spillways serve to choose where the water will go in case of a very big flood, rather than letting the levee give way where there are homes.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/france-levees-loire/

### 6. Takamaka Dam

*It uses mountain water to produce electricity in a power plant hidden underground.*

Location : La Réunion · Photo credit : © BETCGB

#### In brief
On the island of La Réunion, in the Indian Ocean, Takamaka is a spectacular hydroelectric site, at the bottom of a **tropical canyon**. Its power plant is hidden **under 300 meters of volcanic rock**, to withstand cyclones.

#### How does it work?
The valley of the Rivière des Marsouins is one of the **rainiest in the world**:
- Two small arch dams hold back the water: **Takamaka I** (15 m) and **Takamaka II** (29 m).
- During cyclones, the river can rise by **10 meters in 2 hours**! To protect the turbines, the plant was **dug underground**, in volcanic rock (basalt).
- The water falls **270 meters** down a vertical shaft, before reaching the turbines.

#### Key facts
- It rains up to **7 to 8 meters of water per year** in the area!
- **Power**: about 44 MW for the two facilities together. Hydropower is one of the island's main sources of renewable electricity.
- Commissioned in 1968 (Takamaka I) and 1989 (Takamaka II).

#### Words to know
- **Cyclone**: a very violent tropical storm, with huge winds and rain.
- **Basalt**: a black rock formed by a volcano.

> **Did you know?**
> The valley is so steep that no vehicle goes down into it! Equipment and EDF technicians get there by a **cable car** that flies over drops of several hundred meters.

#### Going further
In January 1966, Cyclone Denise dropped more than 1,800 millimeters of rain on La Réunion in 24 hours, a world record: almost three years of Paris rainfall in a single day! That is why the river can rise so fast, and why engineers put the power plant under the mountain, out of harm's way.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/france-takamaka/

## Through time

*2,000 years of water history: from Roman Antiquity to the canals of the 21st century.*

### 1. Pont du Gard

*Built by the Romans 2,000 years ago, it carried the canal that brought water from Uzès to Nîmes over 50 km.*

Location : Occitanie (Gard) · Period : 1st century AD · Photo credit : © Gérard Degoutte

#### In brief
The Pont du Gard is **2,000 years old**! Built by the Romans, it is **the tallest Roman aqueduct bridge in the world**. An *aqueduct* is a structure that carries water, sometimes over tens of kilometers. The Pont du Gard is the part that crosses the river: a *bridge-aqueduct*. It is listed by UNESCO, the list of treasures to protect for all humans.

#### How does it work?
It was not a bridge for cars or carts, but **a piece of a long 50-km canal** that brought water to the Roman city of Nîmes:
- It crosses the Gardon River to bring water from the Eure spring, near Uzès, to the **fountains, baths and homes** of Nîmes.
- **Three levels of arches**: 6 on the first level, 11 on the second, 35 at the very top, which carry the canal.
- **An ultra-precise slope**: over 50 km, the water drops only **12.6 meters** in all. There is no pump: the water flows by itself, very gently, as on a very slight slope!

#### Key facts
- **Height**: 48.77 m. **Length**: 275 m for the preserved upper part (it was longer originally).
- Built in the 1st century, with **stone blocks largely laid dry**, without mortar; the upper channel was covered with a coating.
- Listed by UNESCO in **1985**.

#### Words to know
- **Aqueduct**: a structure that carries water.
- **Mortar**: a mix that glues stones together.

> **Did you know?**
> The inside of the channel was covered with a red coating made of **lime and crushed tiles** to make it watertight. Two thousand years later, you can still see it on the walls!

#### Going further
- The bridge-aqueduct is part of the Nîmes aqueduct, about 50 km long.
- The masonry is largely assembled dry, but the channel and some elements include mortars and coatings.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/temps-pont-du-gard/

### 2. Canal du Midi

*Built in the 17th century, it links the Garonne to the Mediterranean over 240 km, thanks to the Saint-Ferréol lake.*

Location : Occitanie · Period : 1667 – 1681 · Photo credit : © Michèle Pinguet

#### In brief
The Canal du Midi links Toulouse to the Mediterranean Sea over **240 kilometers**. It was imagined by **Pierre-Paul Riquet** in the time of Louis XIV. Its secret: a **big reservoir lake**, Saint-Ferréol, held back by one of the oldest large dams still in service in France.

#### How does it work?
Since Antiquity, people had dreamed of linking the Atlantic to the Mediterranean without going around Spain. But there was a big problem: how to feed with water the **highest point of the canal** (at 189 m altitude) in the middle of summer?
- Riquet had the idea of collecting the water of the streams of the **Montagne Noire** and storing it in a big lake, **Saint-Ferréol**, closed by a 780-meter dam.
- When the canal runs short of water in summer, the gates are opened: the water goes down a **channel** (a small canal, a *rigole*) to the high point.
- Later, Vauban, a famous military engineer, inspected and strengthened the structure.

#### Key facts
- The main canal is **240 km** long and has **65 locks**, including the famous staircase locks of Fonséranes, at Béziers.
- **Saint-Ferréol**: 780 m long, 32 m high.
- Listed by UNESCO in **1996**.

#### Words to know
- **Rigole**: a small canal that brings water.
- **Summit (seuil)**: the highest point where two slopes meet.

> **Did you know?**
> Pierre-Paul Riquet spent all his fortune and his health on his canal. He died in 1680, **a few months before the canal opened**: he never saw it finished!

#### Going further
Saint-Ferréol does not fill up on its own. A network of channels, dug on the slopes of the Montagne Noire, collects the water of several streams and leads it to the lake. Another channel then brings the water to the highest point of the canal.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/temps-canal-du-midi/

### 3. Zola Dam

*Built in the 19th century, it is one of the very first scientifically calculated arch dams.*

Location : Le Tholonet (Aix-en-Provence) · Period : 1847 – 1854 · Photo credit : © Camille Moirenc

#### In brief
At the foot of Mont Sainte-Victoire, near Aix-en-Provence, the Zola dam is a **monument in the history of dams**. It was designed by the engineer **François Zola**, the father of the writer Émile Zola. It is one of the **first arch dams calculated with mathematics**.

#### How does it work?
In a time marked by cholera epidemics (a serious disease caused by dirty water), Aix needed cleaner and more plentiful water. François Zola proposed a dam:
- Instead of a very heavy stone wall, he **calculated** the shape of a stone arch, turned toward the lake.
- The push of the water is sent onto the **two rocky banks** of the gorge.
- François Zola died during the work, in 1847, but his plans were followed to the letter until the inauguration, in 1854.

#### Key facts
- **Height**: 36.5 m. **Length**: 66 m.
- The Zola canal was Aix's main water source until 1877; after that, the Verdon canal took over.

#### Words to know
- **Cholera**: a serious disease spread by dirty water.
- **Strength of materials**: the science that calculates what a stone or a concrete can bear.

> **Did you know?**
> When he was a child, Émile Zola walked near his father's dam with his friend **Paul Cézanne**, who would become a great painter. They swam there, and Cézanne even painted the dam!

#### Going further
At that time, people mostly built thick, heavy walls, relying on experience. François Zola, instead, used calculation to find the shape of an arch that sends the push of the water toward the rocky banks. Today, engineers follow the same reasoning with computers.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/temps-barrage-zola/

### 4. Dardennes Dam

*Near Toulon, it stores water and helps supply the city with drinking water.*

Location : Le Revest-les-Eaux (Var) · Period : 1910 – 1912 · Photo credit : © NGE

#### In brief
At the foot of Mont Caume, at Le Revest-les-Eaux, the Dardennes dam is **Toulon's hundred-year-old water tower**. Built just before the First World War, it still helps supply Toulon and its area with drinking water.

#### How does it work?
It is a **gravity dam** in masonry and concrete, slightly curved:
- It blocks the little river **the Las**, and collects the water of very abundant springs (those of the Ragas and the Foux), which come out of a limestone rock full of holes (called *karstic*).
- The water of the Revest lake is sent nearby, to a plant where it is **filtered** and **disinfected with ozone** before being distributed.
- Between 2020 and 2022, the outlets and the watertightness of the dam were renovated so that it can serve for the next century too.

#### Key facts
- **Height**: 31.6 m. **Crest length**: 154 m.
- **Reservoir**: 1.1 million cubic meters.
- Built between 1910 and 1912.

#### Words to know
- **Karstic**: said of a limestone rock hollowed out by water into holes and passages.
- **Ozone**: a gas used to disinfect water.

> **Did you know?**
> In karstic rock, **whole rivers can flow underground**: the water disappears into a hole, travels in the dark, then sometimes comes out several kilometers away, as a spring.

#### Going further
A karstic rock is like a sponge full of holes: rain soaks in quickly and comes out lower down in very abundant springs. But the water is hardly filtered by the rock, and it can carry impurities. That is why Dardennes water is filtered and disinfected with ozone before it reaches the taps.

### 5. Rizzanese Dam

*Commissioned in 2013 in Corsica, it produces electricity for the whole island's grid.*

Location : Corse-du-Sud (Levie / Sorbollano) · Period : 2007 – 2012 · Photo credit : © Bruno Conty

#### In brief
Inaugurated in 2013 in Corse-du-Sud, the Rizzanese dam is the **most powerful hydroelectric facility in Corsica**. It is one of the last large dams built in France.

#### How does it work?
It is a very modern **gravity dam**, made of **roller-compacted concrete (RCC)**: an almost dry concrete, spread in layers and crushed by rollers:
- This technique makes it possible to build **very fast**.
- The water, held at 530 m altitude, crosses the mountain in a **5.7 km tunnel**, then rushes down a pipe to the Sainte-Lucie-de-Tallano power plant.
- Corsica is an island: its electricity grid is not directly connected to that of mainland France, but it has links with Italy and Sardinia. The Rizzanese turbines, which start very fast, help **balance the grid** morning and evening, when everyone uses a lot of electricity.

#### Key facts
- **Height**: 40.5 m. **Length**: 140 m.
- **Power**: 55 MW, or about 80 million kWh per year.
- It increased by about **40%** Corsica's hydropower production capacity.

#### Words to know
- **Electricity grid**: all the wires and power plants that deliver electricity everywhere.
- **Power and energy**: power (in MW) is measured at one moment; energy produced (in kWh) is measured over a period of time.

> **Did you know?**
> The construction took measures to protect Corsica's native **trout**, with a guaranteed minimum flow of water in the river.

#### Going further
On an island, the electricity grid is small and not well connected to others: at every moment, the amount of electricity produced must equal the amount used, otherwise the grid goes out of balance. A hydropower plant can change its output within minutes. It is therefore valuable to make up for an evening peak in demand, or a cloud that makes solar panel output drop.

### 6. Seine-Nord Europe Canal

*Under construction, it will link the Oise to the Dunkirk-Scheldt canal so that more boats can travel.*

Location : Hauts-de-France · Period : 2022 – 2032 · Photo credit : © CSNE

#### In brief
The Seine-Nord Europe canal is a **very large waterway project** of this century in Europe. **107 kilometers** long, it will link Compiègne to the Dunkirk-Scheldt canal, and connect France to 20,000 km of waterways in northern Europe.

#### How does it work?
It is a big project full of modern ideas:
- **A big canal**: 54 m wide and 4.5 m deep, for convoys of **4,400 tonnes**.
- **6 big locks with "water-saving basins"** (plus a junction lock with the Canal du Nord): these basins recover a large part of the water at each boat passage, up to about **70%**, so as not to drain the neighboring rivers.
- **A reservoir lake**: at Louette, it will hold 14 million cubic meters of water to feed the canal, even during a heatwave.

#### Key facts
- **Length**: 107 km.
- Each big convoy will replace **220 trucks** on the A1 motorway.
- Work started in **2022**, entry into service planned for **2032**.

#### Words to know
- **Water-saving basin**: a reservoir used to save the water of a lock.
- **Large-gauge canal**: said of a canal big enough for large boats.

> **Did you know?**
> The canal will pass over the Somme valley on a **1.3 km canal bridge**, perched on pillars: animals and rivers will be able to keep passing underneath.

#### Going further
The principle of the water-saving basins is simple. When a boat goes down, part of the lock's water first flows into big neighboring basins, with no pump at all, simply thanks to the slope. When a boat goes up, this water is given back to the lock. The water still missing is replaced by pumping.

Fact sheet : https://jul1n.github.io/7familles/en/card/temps-canal-seine-nord/
